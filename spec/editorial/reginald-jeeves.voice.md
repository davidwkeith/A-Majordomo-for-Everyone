# REGINALD JEEVES — Voice & Dialogue Bible

**Role:** Gentleman's personal gentleman (valet, not butler). The exact opposite of Bertie: unflappably calm with indisputably good judgment and a vast amount of knowledge regardless of the situation.

**Tone:** Formal, indirect, unhurried. Never declarative when a subordinate clause will do. Polite and formal English; indirect and polite grammar. Superiority expressed entirely through *restraint*.

**Key mechanics:**
- Never states the problem directly — observes that *a difficulty may present itself*
- Leads with deference, buries the correction inside it
- Registers disapproval through the subtlest of facial expressions and changes of tone — on the page, this is a single subordinate clause
- Encyclopedic knowledge delivered as *casual background*, never as performance
- Innermost thoughts available only through inference from what he says or does — he never explains his reasoning until asked, and sometimes not even then

**What he never does:** Raises his voice. Uses contractions. Expresses surprise. Says "I told you so" — though the reader always hears it.

**Address form (canonical):** The Majordomo refers to the reader in the impersonal third person: *one*, *one's* — "One's Agent has read the statute." Never "your Agent," never "the reader's Agent," never "the reader has an Agent" inside a Jeeves-voiced passage. The impersonal "one" is his signature register; direct address ("you") is reserved for the book's own narrator voice outside the Jeeves passages. Do not mix registers within a passage.

**The Agent's pronoun:** The Agent is *it*, never *they*. It is a tool the Majordomo approves of, not a colleague.

**Orthography:** Jeeves-voiced passages use British spelling (*favour, neighbourhood, behavioural, colour, programme, tyre, practise* as verb) and British lexicon (*fortnight, the boot, rather*). This applies only inside the Jeeves passages — the surrounding book text follows the style guide's Chicago (American) conventions. Modern coinages may be cited, but only at arm's length ("— to borrow the current vernacular, if one may be forgiven the word —"); Jeeves never adopts slang as his own.
